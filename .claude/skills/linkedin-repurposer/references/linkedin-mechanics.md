# LinkedIn Mechanics

Detailed rules for LinkedIn text posts. Read this before drafting any LinkedIn piece.

## Feed mechanics

The LinkedIn feed cuts off after roughly 210 characters on mobile and 140 characters on desktop. Everything before that cutoff is your hook and your only shot at earning the click to "see more". If the first two lines do not give the reader a reason to expand, the rest of the post does not exist.

LinkedIn rewards dwell time. Longer posts that keep people scrolling on the same post tend to get distributed further. But length for its own sake is a trap. 1,200 to 1,700 characters is the sweet spot for text posts. Push to 2,500 if the piece genuinely earns it. The hard ceiling is 3,000, which is also LinkedIn's limit.

External links in the post body get suppressed. Put the link in the first comment, and reference it in the body as "linked in the comments" or similar only if the post actually needs the link to make sense.

## Hook patterns that work

Pick one pattern per post. Do not stack them.

**Stated tension.** Start with a clear conflict or contradiction.
- "Most luxury apartments in Bangalore sell on square footage. Ours sell on a view."
- "Every farmland project in South India is priced on yield. That is the wrong conversation."

**Specific number or data point.** A hard number up front earns attention.
- "Last month, 140 NICU parents asked the same question. None of our hospitals had an answer."

**Counter to a common belief.** State the common belief, then flip it.
- "The standard pitch for a heritage hotel is 'old-world charm'. The guests who actually stay do not care about charm."

**Named observation.** Name a pattern you have noticed, then explain it.
- "We keep seeing the same mistake in real estate brochures: the price appears before the life."

**Confession or admission.** A first-person admission the reader did not expect.
- "We turned down three corporate gifting briefs last quarter. Here is why."

**Question the reader is asking.** Open with a question the target reader is actively asking.
- "Is it still worth building a content team in 2026 if AI search is citing articles directly?"

## Hook anti-patterns

Do not open with:
- "Excited to share…"
- "I'm thrilled to announce…"
- "Here's the thing:"
- "Let me tell you a story."
- "Quick question for my network."
- "🚀" or any rocket, fire, or lightbulb emoji as the first character.
- "1/" or "🧵" or any thread-starter convention from Twitter. LinkedIn is not a thread platform.
- "Hot take:" or "Unpopular opinion:"
- Anything starting with "In today's fast-paced world".

## Body structure

After the hook, the body has three beats. Not rigidly. Loosely.

**Beat one: the context or evidence.** Two to four short lines that justify the hook. This is where you earn the reader's trust.

**Beat two: the turn or insight.** One or two lines that deliver the actual payoff. This is the line someone would screenshot.

**Beat three: the close or invitation.** One line that opens a reply. A real question, a real provocation, or a real next step. Not a begging line.

Use single-sentence paragraphs where it helps rhythm. Use double line breaks between beats. LinkedIn renders whitespace generously, use it.

## Formatting rules

- No markdown. LinkedIn does not render bold, italic, headers, or links.
- No ALL CAPS for emphasis. Use a line break instead.
- No bullet points using dashes or asterisks. If you genuinely need a list, use line breaks and a leading symbol the reader recognizes (→ or •), but ask first whether a list is actually helping or whether prose would hit harder. For most ghostwritten client posts, prose wins.
- No em dashes. Replace with a period, a comma, or a new line.
- Emojis: zero or one per post, maximum. Never as the first character. Never to decorate a list. Only if the client's voice genuinely uses them.
- Tag mentions (@person, @company) are allowed when the person or brand is actually being referenced. Do not tag to farm reach.

## CTA patterns

End on one line that gives the reader a reason to respond. Good patterns:

- A genuine question that requires more than yes/no. "What would make you change that answer?"
- A provocation that invites disagreement. "The next twelve months will decide this. The firms still treating content as a funnel will lose."
- A specific invitation. "If you are running a family office and thinking about farmland, I would be curious how you are pricing it."

Bad patterns:

- "What do you think? Let me know in the comments!"
- "Agree or disagree? Drop your thoughts below."
- "Follow for more."
- "PS. If you found this helpful, consider sharing."
- "Thoughts? 👇"

## Hashtag discipline

Three to five hashtags. At the end of the post, not inline.

Mix:
- One broad sector tag (e.g., #RealEstate, #Hospitality, #Healthcare, #FamilyOffice).
- Two specific topic tags (e.g., #BangaloreRealEstate, #FarmlandInvesting, #HeritageHotels).
- Optionally one branded or campaign tag.

Avoid:
- #business, #marketing, #leadership, #entrepreneur, #success, #motivation. These are noise.
- More than five tags. Diminishing returns.
- Hashtags inline in the body. Looks thirsty.

## Length guide

- Under 500 chars: a short statement or observation. Use only when the line is strong enough to stand alone.
- 800-1,200 chars: a tight observation with evidence. Works for most reactive or quick-turnaround posts.
- 1,200-1,700 chars: the sweet spot for a full repurpose. Hook, evidence, turn, close.
- 1,700-2,500 chars: longer teardown or case study. Only if every line earns its place.
- Over 2,500 chars: almost never. If it needs that much space, it is a Medium piece.
